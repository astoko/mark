# Estuary — research dossier

*Estuary* is a nocturne for piano, hand-written note by note: 66 bars in 6/8, E major, about 3 minutes 15 seconds. This dossier lists the 45 papers I read before composing it, what each one actually says, and where that finding shaped the score.

Every paper below was checked to exist, with the right title, venue and year, and each has a link. For the five papers whose numbers the piece depends on, the parameters come from the full text: Huron 2001, Todd 1992, Repp 1998, Goebl 2001, and the KTH overview (Friberg, Bresin & Sundberg 2006). The rest are used at the level of their published findings.

---

## The idea

An estuary is where a river meets the sea, and fresh water mixes with salt. The piece has two themes:

- **The River** (Theme A) is in E major and sits in the right hand.
  - Its falling lines open with a rising major sixth, and it sighs through appoggiaturas.
  - It is built as a Classical *sentence* inside a *period*: a half cadence in bar 12 and a perfect cadence in bar 19.
- **The Sea** (Theme B) is in C♯ minor and is sung by the left-hand thumb in the tenor, slow as a tide.
  - The river's sixths ripple above it on the offbeats.

Between them lies the **brackish water**: a hexatonic cycle, G♯m → E → Em → C → Cm → A♭ → G♯m. Each chord change moves exactly one voice by a semitone, and major and minor alternate like fresh and salt water. The River's motif sinks by major thirds through it (B → G → E♭ → B) while the Sea answers below. In bar 37, A♭ minor turns out to be G♯ minor (iii of home), and the music surges back.

At the **golden section** (bar 41; 61.2% of the sounding duration) the two themes finally sound together.

- The River is in octaves above.
- The Sea is in the tenor, entering one eighth-note late, so that both remain audible as separate lines.

The coda sings the Sea theme high and in major, then closes with a rarely used "uncanny" cadence (Cohn's *hexatonic pole*): A → Am → **C minor → E major**, where all three upper voices move by one semitone. It ends on the falling bell-notes of the opening.

## Where the piece bends the rules on purpose

- **Remote but smooth.** The development visits C and A♭, harmonies far from E by circle-of-fifths distance (high tension per Lerdahl & Krumhansl 2007), yet every step moves a single semitone (Cohn). It sounds strange and inevitable at once.
- **A quiet peak of distance.** Harmonic tension peaks while the dynamics stay low ("misterioso"): Farbood's tension parameters are set against each other, and they only line up in the surge of bars 38–40.
- **An uncommon ending.** The final cadence is the hexatonic pole (C minor → E major), which Cohn associates with the uncanny, placed after a conventional minor-plagal sigh.
- **Counterpoint by timing.** The confluence keeps two tunes distinct mainly by offset onsets and register, following Huron's principles, rather than by academic species rules.
- **Proportion measured in seconds.** The golden section is placed in time as the listener hears it, rather than in bar numbers.
